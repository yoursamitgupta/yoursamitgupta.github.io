How I lead

Mentor first. Title second.

I currently guide a 25-person SDET organization. My job is not to be the bottleneck or the boss in the room — it is to leave people stronger than I found them.

What I refuse to be

  • The person who only asks “is it done?”
  • The reviewer who rewrites your PR instead of teaching the why
  • The manager who hoards context so the team stays dependent
  • The hero who jumps in and steals the learning

What I practice

  • Ask better questions so engineers find the answer themselves
  • Pair on hard bugs, then step back so they own the next one
  • Share the map: quality strategy, gates, and career paths in the open
  • Give credit in public, feedback in private, growth on a timeline
01

Teach the system, not the ticket

Anyone can assign work. I explain how a quality gate, a CI pipeline, or a domain risk connects to the product. Context is how juniors become architects.

02

Psychological safety is a quality tool

People hide flaky tests and production scares when they fear blame. Safe teams surface risk early. That is not “soft leadership” — it is defect prevention.

03

Grow 25 people, not 1 hero

If only I can design the framework, I have failed. I invest in tech leads, pairing, and written standards so the org scales without me in every review.

04

Stay technical enough to be useful

Mentors who cannot open a Playwright trace or a Kubernetes log lose trust. I still write, still debug, still ship OSS — so advice is earned, not delegated.

Takeaways

Field notes you can steal

Open a card. These are practices I use with SDET teams — written so you can try them this week, whether you lead two people or twenty-five.

When someone is stuck, do not grab the keyboard. Walk this order:

  1. What did you expect to happen? Forces a hypothesis.
  2. What actually happened? Separates story from evidence.
  3. Where is the first place that disagrees? Log, assertion, network, data.
  4. What would you try if I were not here? Builds ownership.

Try this: in your next 1:1 debug, you only ask questions for the first 10 minutes. Offer a hint only after they have a plan.

A release gate that always fails is ignored. A gate with no signal is theater. Design three layers:

  1. Fast: lint, unit, contract tests on every PR (minutes).
  2. Honest: critical user journeys + API health before merge.
  3. Deep: performance, security, and data jobs on a cadence — not blocking every commit.

Try this: list every check in CI. Mark each as signal, noise, or unknown. Delete or quarantine noise this sprint.

Shift-left fails when QA is invited after the design is frozen. Bring quality into the room earlier:

  1. In planning, ask: what would make this feature unsafe to ship?
  2. Turn that answer into a testable example before code exists.
  3. Automate the example, not a 40-step UI script that nobody trusts.

Try this: pick one in-flight story. Write the failure condition in one sentence and add it as an automated check before the feature is “dev complete”.

Ambition without a path becomes burnout. For each engineer I mentor, we keep a one-page map:

  1. Now: what they already do well (evidence, not vibes).
  2. Next: one skill that would change their scope in 90 days.
  3. Practice: a real project they will own — framework module, quality gate, or domain deep-dive.

Try this: ask someone you mentor, “What should you be trusted with in three months that you are not trusted with today?” Then give them that work on purpose.

AI can write tests, heal selectors, and summarize failures. It cannot know your product truth unless you define it.

  1. Keep a human-owned expected result (schema, KPI, invariant).
  2. Use LLMs to propose checks, then lock them as contracts (this is why I built PromptSpec).
  3. Evaluate RAG and agent answers the same way you evaluate APIs — with fixtures, not vibes.

Try this: take one AI-generated test. Ask “what would make this pass while the product is still wrong?” Fix that oracle first.

Teams copy what leaders celebrate. If you only praise firefighting, you get more fires.

  1. Celebrate the boring win: a flake deleted, a gate that caught a real bug, a junior who unblocked themselves.
  2. Give hard feedback one-to-one, with an example and a next experiment — never as a personality verdict.
  3. When you were wrong, say so first. Mentors who never err teach people to hide errors.

Try this: in the next standup, thank someone for prevention (a test, a doc, a catch) instead of a late-night hero save.
